# Literature Review: International Agreements as a Tool for Overcoming Domestic Constraints on U.S. Executive Authority (1980–2017)

*Prepared 2026-11-04. Every entry in Section 4 was checked against Crossref, OpenAlex, a publisher or journal page, or a repository record. Items that could not be checked are listed in Section 5. Author-year citations in the text match Section 4.*

---

## 1. Summary: state of the field and the gap

**Claim under study.** When presidents face domestic resistance from Congress, courts, states or interest groups, they negotiate and sign international agreements partly to gain or shore up the authority they need to regulate and enforce preferred policies at home. The archives should show (1) negotiators discussing problems of domestic implementation and (2) officials explaining how the agreement helps solve them.

**Theory is strong.** Putnam's (1988) two-level game already allows a chief negotiator to use international talks to change what is possible at home ("synergistic linkage," "reverberation"). Moravcsik (1994) makes the executive-empowerment version explicit. International cooperation lets executives "cut slack" from legislatures and interest groups through four channels: control of initiative, institutions, information and ideas. Wolf (1999) and Koenig-Archibugi (2004) generalize this as a "new raison d'état." Related mechanisms are well theorized and have been tested outside the U.S.:
- external conditionality and scapegoating (Vreeland 2003; Allee & Huth 2006);
- lock-in against future governments (Moravcsik 2000; Pevehouse 2005);
- empowering domestic allies (Dai 2005, 2007; Simmons 2009; Keck & Sikkink 1998);
- venue shopping (Baumgartner & Jones 1993; Guiraudon 2000; Pralle 2003).

Nzelibe (2011) offers the closest U.S.-specific legal-theoretical statement. He argues that incumbents use international legal commitments "to overcome domestic obstacles to their narrow policy and electoral objectives," and he uses NAFTA as a case.

**U.S. institutional work is mostly about the form of agreements, counted at scale.** Political scientists have built large datasets on the choice between Article II treaties and executive agreements, Senate behavior, and the rise of unilateral instruments (Krutz & Peake 2006, 2009; Martin 2005; Caruson & Farrar-Myers 2007; Auerswald & Maltzman 2003; Kelley & Pevehouse 2015; Peake 2014, 2023). Legal scholars document the presidency's growing control over making, interpreting and terminating international law (Hathaway 2008, 2009; Bradley & Goldsmith 2018; Galbraith 2017; Hathaway, Bradley & Goldsmith 2020; Bradley, Goldsmith & Hathaway 2022). Ingber (2016) names an "empowerment phenomenon," in which the executive invokes international law as a *source* of authority rather than a limit. Bodansky & Spiro (2016) describe "executive agreements plus," which are implemented through existing statutory authority, as with Paris and the Clean Air Act.

These works focus on **which instrument** presidents choose and **whether** that choice evades Congress. They do not ask how an agreement's **content** is designed to supply domestic implementation or enforcement authority.

**Direct archival evidence of intent is thin, which confirms the expected gap with some revision.** Process-tracing and negotiator accounts show that U.S. negotiators constantly discuss ratification and domestic politics. Examples include Panama (Moffett 1985; Clymer 2008), NAFTA (Mayer 1998; Cameron & Tomlin 2000), Montreal (Benedick 1991/1998; Parson 2003), arms control (Krepon & Caldwell 1991; Kreps, Saunders & Schultz 2018), Kyoto (Hovi, Sprinz & Bang 2010) and the Iran deal (Hurst 2016). But that discussion is mostly about **constraint**: winning votes, side payments, reservations. Only a handful of studies show executives using agreements **to create or enforce domestic authority** against resistance:
- **Brewster (2017):** the 1997 OECD Anti-Bribery Convention unlocked aggressive enforcement of the FCPA by neutralizing business objections about competitiveness.
- **DeSombre (2000):** the U.S. internationalizes its own environmental regulations to satisfy coalitions of environmentalists and industry.
- **Reagan Library records on Montreal** (published in NSArchive Briefing Book #868, 2024): officials warned that without an international agreement "the courts would compel EPA to impose unilateral reductions."
- **Gawthorpe (2026, *PSQ*):** a new concept of "presidential extra-territorialization / jurisdictional arbitrage" through executive agreements. It is the closest new competitor, but it is about evading rights-based constraints, and its evidence is three illustrative cases rather than systematic archival coding.

**The gap this project fills.** No study systematically codes presidential-library records across administrations (Carter–Obama) and issue areas for:
- (a) explicit discussion of domestic implementation obstacles from Congress, courts, states and interest groups during negotiation; and
- (b) explicit statements that the agreement, its form or its content will supply authority, preemption, enforcement leverage or political cover to overcome them.

Large-N studies measure form, not intent. Single-case histories describe intent for one agreement. Computational work on diplomatic archives (Connelly et al. 2020, 2021; Chaney et al. 2016; Min 2020) has not been aimed at this question. This project's LLM-assisted coding of OCR'd presidential-library files, guided by the mechanism scheme in Section 3, would be new in method and in substance.

**Revisions to the expected gap:**
- (i) Some of the gap is already partly filled for single issue areas: anti-bribery (Brewster 2017), the environment (DeSombre 2000; Kelemen & Vogel 2010), and trade (Goldstein 1996; Nzelibe 2011). The project should position itself as generalizing and testing these across administrations.
- (ii) "Courts" and "states" are the least-studied sources of resistance in the political-science work on agreements. They are mainly covered by legal scholarship on the treaty power and self-execution (Bradley 1998; Yoo 1999).
- (iii) The project must code **counter-evidence**: agreements that executives designed to *avoid* creating domestic obligations, such as non-self-executing declarations and political commitments. Otherwise selection on the dependent variable is a serious risk (Lustick 1996).

## 2. Synthesized discussion

### Q1. How international commitments change domestic bargaining power and executive autonomy

**Two-level games and synergistic linkage.** Putnam (1988) models a chief negotiator who bargains internationally (Level I) while needing domestic ratification (Level II). The overlap of the two sides' "win-sets" determines whether a deal is possible. The features most relevant to this project are:
- **Synergistic issue linkage:** an international package makes feasible a domestic policy that lacked a majority on its own. Putnam's example is the 1978 Bonn summit, studied in Putnam & Bayne (1984).
- **Reverberation:** foreign pressure that shifts domestic opinion.
- **The negotiator's own preferences:** the negotiator may welcome foreign pressure to defeat domestic opponents.

Evans, Jacobson & Putnam (1993) apply the framework in eleven case studies, including Moravcsik's (1993) chapter on transatlantic arms collaboration. Evans's (1993) concluding chapter reports that chief negotiators often acted to pursue their own domestic agendas, not simply as agents of their constituents. That finding directly supports the executive-empowerment reading.

Schoppa (1993) clarifies the conditions under which foreign pressure (*gaiatsu*) works. Pressure succeeds when it widens domestic participation or opens new policy alternatives, not when it only tries to change minds.

Formal extensions treat domestic ratifiers and uncertainty explicitly:
- Iida (1993) on uncertainty;
- Mo (1995) on agent veto;
- Milner & Rosendorff (1997) on divided government;
- Tarar (2001) on two-sided constraints.

Milner (1997) is the canonical account of how the distribution of power and information between executive and legislature shapes cooperation. Divided government reduces the chances of agreement. Interest groups can act as "endorsers" that inform the legislature.

**"Cutting slack" and the new raison d'état.** Moravcsik (1994) reverses the usual logic. Rather than being constrained by domestic ratifiers, executives use international cooperation to shift four domestic resources in their own favor:
- **Initiative:** control of the agenda.
- **Institutions:** decision procedures, such as take-it-or-leave-it ratification.
- **Information:** informational asymmetries.
- **Ideas:** new justifications for policy.

Moravcsik develops this for the EC, but the logic carries over: a U.S. executive negotiating an agreement controls the text, presents Congress with a package, holds the negotiating record, and can invoke international obligation. Wolf (1999) and Koenig-Archibugi (2004) develop the same idea as a "new raison d'état," in which governments move policy into intergovernmental venues to gain autonomy from domestic control.

**Tying hands, credible commitment and lock-in.** Schelling (1960) argued that a negotiator who is constrained at home can extract better terms. Putnam (1988) calls this the Schelling conjecture, and Evans et al. (1993) found mixed support for it. Fearon (1997) distinguishes "tying hands" from "sinking costs" as signaling strategies, and Schultz (1998) shows how domestic opposition makes signals credible.

For this project, the key form is **lock-in**: binding future governments or domestic opponents to policies the current executive prefers.
- Moravcsik (2000) shows that governments in new democracies supported binding human-rights regimes to lock in domestic policy against future opponents.
- Pevehouse (2005) and Mansfield & Pevehouse (2006) extend the logic to regional organizations.
- Martin (2000, 2005) argues that legislative involvement increases the credibility of commitments. Presidents therefore choose treaties over executive agreements when credibility matters.

**Scapegoating, external conditionality and political cover.** Vreeland (2003) shows that governments sometimes *seek* IMF programs to gain leverage over domestic veto players for unpopular reforms. The outside conditions become a bargaining asset at home. Allee & Huth (2006) show that leaders take territorial disputes to international legal bodies to gain "domestic political cover" for concessions. The U.S. analogue is an administration that says an international obligation "requires" a regulatory step it already favors.

**Empowering domestic allies and transnational coalitions.**
- Dai (2005, 2007) theorizes a "domestic constituency mechanism": international institutions supply information and standards that empower domestic groups to pressure their own governments.
- Simmons (2009) shows that treaties change domestic politics by setting agendas, supporting litigation and enabling mobilization.
- Keck & Sikkink (1998) describe the "boomerang" pattern, in which blocked domestic actors use transnational networks.

These works usually picture society pressuring the state. Turned around, an executive can deliberately empower allied agencies, NGOs and firms through an agreement.

**Leveling the playing field and internationalizing domestic regulation.** DeSombre (2000) shows that the U.S. tries to internationalize domestic environmental regulation when environmentalists and regulated industry both favor it. Industry wants foreign competitors bound by the same rules, so internationalization **defuses business opposition** to domestic enforcement. Two studies make the same argument for anti-corruption:
- Abbott & Snidal (2002) on the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention.
- Brewster (2017), who shows that the 1997 Convention was critical to the post-2000 expansion of FCPA enforcement because it allowed "competition-neutral" prosecution.

Kelemen & Vogel (2010) explain shifts in U.S. and EU environmental leadership as an effort to export domestic regulatory standards. Kelemen & Knievel (2015) extend this to the domestic constitutional side.

**Venue shopping.** Baumgartner & Jones (1993) show that actors who lose in one venue seek another. Guiraudon (2000) applies this to "vertical" venue shopping, in which interior ministries moved migration policy to EU forums to escape domestic courts and legislatures. Pralle (2003) shows advocacy groups internationalizing a dispute. This is the closest conceptual match to presidents moving policy into international negotiation when Congress, courts or states block domestic action. Gawthorpe's (2026) "jurisdictional arbitrage" is a U.S.-presidential version.

**Side payments and signaling.**
- Putnam (1988) emphasizes targeted side payments to build domestic ratification coalitions. Evans (2004) documents pork-barrel side payments used to pass NAFTA in Congress.
- Kreps, Saunders & Schultz (2018) identify a "ratification premium": presidents pay concessions to hawkish senators to ratify arms-control treaties.
- Mansfield, Milner & Rosendorff (2002) argue that trade agreements help democratic leaders signal policy to voters. Mansfield, Milner & Pevehouse (2007) show that veto players reduce PTA formation.
- Chaudoin (2014) shows experimentally that international agreements change how audiences react to policy.

**Design to manage domestic constraints.**
- Lipson (1991) and Abbott & Snidal (2000) explain informal and soft-law agreements partly as a way to avoid ratification hurdles.
- Rosendorff & Milner (2001) explain escape clauses as insurance against domestic shocks.
- Goldstein (1996) and Goldstein & Martin (2000) show that legalized dispute settlement changes domestic institutions and coalitions, for example the binational panels in CUSFTA/NAFTA.
- Bailey, Goldstein & Weingast (1997), Hiscox (1999), Lohmann & O'Halloran (1994) and O'Halloran (1994) explain how Congress delegated trade-agreement authority to the executive (RTAA, fast track) so that international commitments could restructure domestic coalitions. This is a historical case of agreements reshaping domestic power.

**Synthesis for the project.** The literature offers at least ten distinct channels (Section 3). Most were developed for the EU, developing countries or trade, and most were tested by outcomes, not by documented intent. The U.S. presidency fits the "cutting slack" model unusually well, because the executive controls negotiation, can choose among treaty, congressional-executive, sole-executive and nonbinding forms, and can implement through agencies.

### Q2. U.S. institutions: treaties, executive agreements, unilateralism and implementation

**Forms of agreement and the "evasion" debate.**
- **Article II treaties** need two-thirds Senate consent.
- **Congressional-executive agreements** are authorized before or approved after by a majority of both houses.
- **Sole executive agreements** rest on the president's Article II powers.
- **Nonbinding political commitments** are increasingly common.

Ackerman & Golove (1995) defend the constitutional interchangeability of congressional-executive agreements and treaties. Spiro (2001) and Yoo (1999) offer competing accounts of constitutional method and self-execution. Setear (2002) models the president's choice of ratification pathway in rational-choice terms.

Krutz & Peake (2006, 2009) give the main political-science account. Executive agreements grew to make up the large majority of U.S. international agreements, but mostly with congressional acquiescence and delegation, as an efficiency adaptation rather than outright evasion. They do find evidence that presidents choose forms strategically on salient agreements.

Other political-science work on form choice and treaty behavior:
- Martin (2005) argues that treaties signal commitment.
- Caruson & Farrar-Myers (2007) study how presidents use executive agreements to advance foreign-policy priorities in relation to congressional support.
- Kelley & Pevehouse (2015) explain treaty transmission and ratification by the opportunity cost of Senate floor time.
- Peake (2014) models bilateral treaty ratification from 1949 to 2012.
- Peake (2023) argues that partisan polarization made the treaty process "dysfunctional," pushing presidents to unilateral tools that are less durable and less legitimate. His cases include climate, Iran, and Iraq/Afghanistan security agreements.
- Nyarko (2019) compares how durable treaties and executive agreements are.
- Roger (2020) explains the global shift toward informal arrangements.
- Ostermann & Wagner (2023) introduce a cross-national database of treaty ratification votes.

**The Senate.** Auerswald & Maltzman (2003) show that the Senate shapes treaties after the fact through reservations, understandings and declarations (RUDs). Presidents anticipate this, which makes RUDs an observable sign of domestic constraint. Lindsay (1994) is the standard account of Congress's indirect and procedural influence on foreign policy. Kreps, Saunders & Schultz (2018) show the bargaining over Senate ratification of arms control.

**Presidential unilateralism.** Moe & Howell (1999a, 1999b) theorize unilateral action. Presidents "move first" and Congress and the courts struggle to reverse them. Howell (2003) tests this with executive orders. Rudalevige (2005) traces the post-Watergate revival of presidential power. Howell & Pevehouse (2007) show that Congress constrains the use of force. Christenson & Kriner (2020) show that public opinion constrains unilateral action. Lowande & Rogowski (2021) review the field. Milner & Tingley (2015) show that presidential influence is greatest in foreign-policy instruments where Congress has less control. That is a reason to expect presidents to steer policy into agreement-based instruments.

**Presidential control over international law.** Hathaway (2008) documents the shift from Article II treaties to executive agreements and argues that congressional-executive agreements should largely replace treaties. Hathaway (2009) argues that presidents have gained unchecked power over international law, partly through broad *ex ante* congressional authorizations that let presidents "bypass the other branches" in pursuing core policy aims. Bradley & Goldsmith (2018) show that the president now largely controls the making, interpretation and termination of U.S. international law. Their examples include Obama-era agreements such as Paris and the Iran nuclear deal.

Related legal work:
- Hathaway, Bradley & Goldsmith (2020) use FOIA-obtained records to show that executive reporting of agreements to Congress under the Case Act is badly incomplete. This matters for any count-based design.
- Bradley, Goldsmith & Hathaway (2022) document the rise of nonbinding agreements.
- Galbraith (2017) argues that international and administrative law now provide checks that partly replace congressional ones. She uses Paris as a case and shows that Paris was designed around what the executive could implement without Congress.
- Bodansky & Spiro (2016) define "executive agreements plus": agreements concluded by the executive and implemented through *existing* statutes. This is the clearest legal description of agreements built to fit existing executive regulatory authority.
- Ingber (2016) documents the executive branch's use of international law to support broad readings of statutory and constitutional authority.
- Kemp (2015, 2016) analyzes how the Paris architecture was "US-proofed" to avoid the Senate.
- Claussen (2017) shows the executive's growing role in trade lawmaking.
- Bradley (2020, 2024) gives a doctrinal overview and a historical-gloss account of these practices.

**Federalism, courts and implementation.**
- Bradley (1998) analyzes the treaty power as a means of federal regulation that might exceed Congress's enumerated powers. The Missouri v. Holland line of cases makes treaties a potential tool against *state* resistance.
- Yoo (1999) argues for non-self-execution, which keeps implementation with Congress.

The self-execution issue is how courts determine whether an agreement gives the executive enforceable domestic authority. It is a likely topic in archival legal-adviser memos.

**Synthesis.** U.S. institutional work establishes that presidents (a) choose agreement form strategically, (b) increasingly act without Congress, and (c) sometimes implement agreements through existing agency authority. It has not established through documents that presidents negotiate agreement content in order to supply domestic authority against specific resistance.

### Q3. Evidence that domestic politics is discussed explicitly during negotiations

**Panama Canal treaties (1977–78, before the 1980 window but the project's pilot).** Moffett (1985) is the standard political-science study of ratification and presidential management of the Senate. Clymer (2008) traces how the fight mobilized the New Right. Hogan (1986) analyzes domestic advocacy. Strong (2000) and Glad (2009) use Carter Library material on how Carter made foreign policy. The project's sample folder (Hamilton Jordan files, June–July 1977) has the expected genres: Senate target lists, "steps to obtain congressional and public support," replies to senators. Most of it is **ratification-constraint** evidence, not evidence of using the agreement to gain authority. The coding scheme should keep these apart.

**NAFTA and the side agreements (1991–93).**
- Mayer (1998) is a multi-level account based on interviews. It shows the Clinton administration negotiated the labor and environment side agreements to build a domestic coalition.
- Cameron & Tomlin (2000) reconstruct the trilateral bargaining.
- Evans (2004) documents vote-buying in the House.
- Nzelibe (2011) reads NAFTA as incumbents using international commitments to entrench domestic policy goals.
- Goldstein (1996) shows how CUSFTA dispute panels changed domestic trade-remedy institutions.

**Montreal Protocol (1985–87).**
- Benedick (1991/1998) is the chief U.S. negotiator's account. It describes the interagency fight in the Reagan administration, including Interior Secretary Hodel's "personal protection" episode, and industry's change of position.
- Parson (2003) offers a scholarly reconstruction.
- DeSombre (2000) explains U.S. leadership as internationalizing domestic CFC regulation.
- Sunstein (2007) compares Montreal and Kyoto.
- NSArchive Briefing Book #868 (2024) publishes Reagan Library documents (Robert Johnson, Beryl Sprinkel and Nancy Risque files) and FRUS 1981–88 vol. XLI documents. These show officials arguing that without an agreement, courts would force unilateral EPA controls that would harm U.S. industry. It is a **near-ideal example** of mechanism M8/M10 in Section 3.
- Militello (2019) frames the Montreal debate as regulatory nationalism versus neo-conservative nationalism.

**Arms control.** Krepon & Caldwell (1991) collect case studies of ratification politics. Caldwell (1991) covers SALT II. Kreps, Saunders & Schultz (2018) show presidents paying a "ratification premium" to hawks. Most of this evidence is about constraint rather than gaining authority.

**Kyoto and Paris.**
- Hovi, Sprinz & Bang (2010) and Bang, Hovi & Sprinz (2012) use interviews to explain U.S. non-ratification of Kyoto and of multilateral environmental agreements generally. They stress the Senate's Byrd-Hagel resolution and the domestic ratification barrier.
- Falkner (2016) explains Paris's "pledge and review" logic as partly an accommodation of U.S. domestic constraints.
- Galbraith (2017), Bodansky & Spiro (2016) and Kemp (2015, 2016) show that Paris was designed so the executive could join and implement under existing Clean Air Act authority. This is the clearest instance of the project's mechanism, but it rests on legal analysis, not archival evidence. Obama Library records are only now becoming available.

**Iran nuclear deal (JCPOA, 2015).** Hurst (2016) analyzes the negotiations as a two-level game and shows that Congress, through the Iran Nuclear Agreement Review Act, nearly derailed the deal. Bradley & Goldsmith (2018) and Peake (2023) treat the JCPOA as a nonbinding political commitment chosen to avoid Senate consent.

**Other.**
- Brewster (2017) shows OECD Anti-Bribery Convention negotiators and DOJ officials expected the treaty to ease domestic enforcement against competitiveness objections.
- Strieff (2015) uses Carter Library records to show how domestic politics shaped Camp David diplomacy.
- Rathbun (2011) uses archival evidence on the "hidden domestic politics" of U.S. multilateralism after World War II.

**Synthesis.** Explicit discussion of domestic politics during negotiation is well documented. The *direction* of most of that evidence is constraint management: votes, side payments, reservations, design concessions. Evidence of the *empowerment* direction comes from a few cases: Montreal, FCPA/OECD, Paris and NAFTA. This asymmetry is the empirical opening.

### Q4. Archival and process-tracing method precedents, and computational work on diplomatic archives

**Archival method in political science.**
- Trachtenberg (2006) is the standard guide to working with diplomatic documents.
- Lustick (1996) warns about selecting among historical records, which matters for an archive-based design.
- Darnton (2018) sets out an inferential framework for documentary evidence: document genre, provenance and declassification bias. He applies it to U.S. entry into World War II.
- Bennett & Checkel (2015) is the reference for process-tracing tests (hoop and smoking-gun).

Studies that use presidential-library records on international bargaining include:
- Yarhi-Milo (2014), which uses Carter- and Reagan-era records among others;
- Strieff (2015) on Carter and Camp David;
- Glad (2009) and Strong (2000) on Carter;
- Kreps, Saunders & Schultz (2018) on arms-control ratification;
- Rathbun (2011).

None of these codes agreement negotiations across administrations for executive-empowerment intent.

**Computational text analysis of diplomatic and presidential archives.**
- Connelly, Hicks, Jervis, Spirling & Suong (2020) introduce History Lab's Freedom of Information Archive (FOIArchive), a structured corpus of declassified U.S. diplomatic documents, including the State Department Central Foreign Policy Files cables, FRUS and other collections.
- Connelly et al. (2021) apply new text methods to the Iranian revolution as an intelligence failure.
- Chaney, Wallach, Connelly & Blei (2016) develop event detection on State Department cables.
- Connelly (2023) uses History Lab's work to study declassification and secrecy.
- Min (2020) uses FRUS-based text analysis to study wartime negotiation in the Korean War.
- Özsoy, Salamanca & Connelly (2023, arXiv preprint) build a knowledge graph of FRUS.
- Kern, Gustafson & Hansen (2026) apply data science to intelligence-failure archives.

For methods:
- Grimmer & Stewart (2013) set out validation principles for text-as-data.
- Gilardi, Alizadeh & Kubli (2023) and Ziems et al. (2024) assess LLM annotation against human coders. They support LLM coding with human-validated subsets, intercoder agreement statistics and published codebooks.

**Gap for method.** The computational work targets cables (State/FRUS) and intelligence questions, not presidential-library White House files on agreement negotiation. As far as this search found, no published work applies LLM coding to OCR'd presidential-library records on international agreements. Presidential-library collections also differ from FRUS: they are not curated, and they include domestic-policy staff (DPC, OMB, Congressional Liaison, Counsel) whose memos are exactly where implementation obstacles appear. FRUS volumes, such as FRUS 1981–88 vol. XLI on global issues, can serve as a curated validation set.

## 3. Mechanism table (coding scheme)

*Use one code per passage. A passage can carry several. Codes M1–M15 are **empowerment** mechanisms, meaning the agreement helps the executive overcome resistance. Code C0 is the **constraint-only** comparison: domestic politics discussed purely as a limit. Coding C0 is essential to avoid selecting on the dependent variable. For each code, record the **source of resistance** (Congress / courts / states / interest groups / bureaucracy / public) and the **resource gained** (legal authority / enforcement leverage / preemption / political cover / coalition / lock-in).*

**Coding notes for LLM-assisted coding.**
- (1) Record document genre: memo, memcon, cable, talking points, C-175 memo, legal opinion, letter, briefing book, handwritten note. Darnton (2018) shows that genre affects how evidence should be weighed.
- (2) Record author office: NSC, DPC, Counsel, Congressional Liaison, OMB, State/L, USTR, EPA, DOJ.
- (3) Require a quoted text span for every code, so that coding can be validated against human coders (Grimmer & Stewart 2013; Ziems et al. 2024).
- (4) Distinguish *anticipated* from *realized* effects.
- (5) Flag OCR confidence.
